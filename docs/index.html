Evaluating the Proximity of Logistics Facilities to Residential Neighbourhoods in the Netherlands Through Dynamical Systems and Monte Carlo Methods

How close are Dutch logistics buildings to the places where people live, and are they closer or farther than if they had been sited at random within industrial estates?

Advanced Mathematics for Business and Technology, Vrije Universiteit Brussel. Individual project. Data: 26,951 logistics buildings (Nefs, 2022) and a 500 m population grid (CBS).

The short answer depends on what “random” means

Median distance from a logistics building to the centre of the nearest 500 m cell with at least 100 inhabitants. Bars show the middle 95% of 2,000 random placements under each null model; the diamond is the observed value.

The verdict flips with the null model

Against estates chosen by area, logistics sits closer to residents than chance. Against estates that are all equally likely, it sits farther (for 5, 100 and 500 inhabitants per cell). A plain “closer than random” is therefore not justified.

Position inside an estate barely matters

Keeping each building in its real estate and moving it at random changes the median by only about 2% (8 m), far below the 500 m resolution of the grid. Proximity is mostly decided by which estate hosts logistics.

Newer buildings are farther from today’s homes

The median distance rises from 286 m for buildings from before 1970 to 512 m for those from 2005 on. This holds against both random baselines, but it describes the buildings that exist now; it does not show that planners chose to move logistics away.

Mathematical background

This section collects the theory behind the two analyses, with the derivations needed to reproduce every number above. Equations are numbered so that they can be referred to.

Part 1. Dynamical systems

Equilibria of an autonomous equation

An autonomous first-order equation has the form N′=f(N): the growth rate depends only on the present state. A value N* with f(N*)=0 is an equilibrium, because the solution stays there. For the logistic law f(N)=rN(1−N/K) there are two:

dNdt=f(N)=rN(1−NK)⇒N*=0 or N*=K
(1)

Stability by linearisation (Taylor expansion)

Expand f around an equilibrium (Chapter 2, Taylor series) and put u=N−N*:

f(N)=f(N*)+f′(N*)u+12f′(ξ)u2,f(N*)=0
(2)

For small u the quadratic term is negligible, so u′≈λu with λ=f′(N*), whose solution is

u(t)=u0eλt
(3)

If λ<0 a small disturbance decays and N* is stable; if λ>0 it grows and N* is unstable. For the logistic law:

f′(N)=r(1−2NK),f′(0)=r>0(unstable),f′(K)=−r<0(stable)
(4)

Around K the expansion is exact with only two terms, f(K+u)=−ru−rKu2, so the error of the linear approximation is (r/K)u2:

f(K+u)=−ru−rKu2
(5)

Solving the equation by separation of variables (Chapter 3)

Separate N and t and use partial fractions:

∫dNN(1−N/K)=∫rdt,1N(1−N/K)=1N+1/K1−N/K
(6)

Integrating gives lnN1−N/K=rt+C. Solving for N yields the S-shaped curve, where t0 is the year in which N=K/2:

N(t)=K1+e−r(t−t0)
(7)

The growth rate f(N) is a parabola in N. It is largest where f′(N)=0, that is at N=K/2 (the inflection point of N(t)):

maxNf(N)=f(K2)=rK4≈0.084·31,0854≈650buildings per year
(8)

Estimating r and K from data

Method B uses the discrete version of the equation. Writing f(N)=rN−(r/K)N2=aN−bN2, the yearly increments satisfy ΔNt≈aNt−bNt2, which is linear in (a,b) and is solved by ordinary least squares; then r=a and K=a/b:

mina,b∑t(ΔNt−aNt+bNt2)2,r=a,K=ab
(9)

Method A fits the closed form (7) to the cumulative counts by non-linear least squares; the standard errors come from the Jacobian J of the model at the optimum and the residual variance s2:

minK,r,t0∑t(Nt−K1+e−r(t−t0))2,Var(θ^)≈s2(JTJ)−1
(10)

Goodness of fit is measured by the coefficient of determination. A monotone cumulative series is smooth by construction, so R2 of method A (0.998) is inflated; the increments of method B (0.69) are the honest benchmark.

R2=1−∑t(yt−y^t)2∑t(yt−y¯)2
(11)

For a system x′=F(x) with several variables, the same recipe uses the Jacobian matrix at an equilibrium. The equilibrium is stable when every eigenvalue has a negative real part. In two dimensions the eigenvalues follow from the trace τ and determinant Δ of J:

J=∂F∂x(x*),λ=τ±τ2−4Δ2,τ=trJ,Δ=detJ

Part 2. Monte Carlo simulation

Estimating an expectation

To approximate θ=𝔼[g(X)], draw independent copies X1,…,XB and average. By the law of large numbers θ^B→θ; its variance is σ2/B, and by the central limit theorem an approximate 95% interval is θ^B±1.96s/B. Halving the error therefore needs four times as many replicates. With B=2000 the standard error of the simulated null means in the 12 overall comparisons is at most 0.16 m.

θ^B=1B∑b=1Bg(Xb),Var(θ^B)=σ2B,θ^B±1.96sB
(13)

The statistic and the three null models

For building i at position xi and residential cells j (those with at least θ inhabitants) centred at cj, the distance and the test statistic are

di=minj:popj≥θ‖xi−cj‖,T=median(d1,…,dn)
(14)

Each null model is a sampling rule for the positions. With estate areas Ak and m=3,001 estates, the probability density of a random point x inside estate k is:

N1a (estate ∝ area):P(k)=Ak∑jAj⇒f(x)=Ak∑jAj·1Ak=1∑jAj
(15)
N1b (estates equally likely):P(k)=1m⇒f(x)=1mAk
(16)
N2 (own estate k(i)):f(x)=1Ak(i)
(17)

So N1a is uniform over the whole area of all estates, N1b favours points in small estates (higher density per square metre), and N2 only reshuffles the position inside the estate a building already has. This is why the three give different answers: they ask different questions.

Uniform random points in a polygon

Estate shapes are irregular, so points are drawn by rejection sampling: draw uniformly in the bounding rectangle R and keep the point if it falls inside the polygon P (and outside any hole). The accepted points are uniform on P, because for any region S⊂P the conditional probability is Area(S)/Area(P); the acceptance probability is

Pr(accept)=Area(P)Area(R)
(18)

The empirical p-value

With the observed statistic Tobs and simulated values T1,…,TB under the null:

pcloser=1+#{b:Tb≤Tobs}B+1,pfarther=1+#{b:Tb≥Tobs}B+1
(19)
p2=min{1,2min(pcloser,pfarther)}
(20)

The "+1" is not a fudge. If the null is true, Tobs,T1,…,TB are exchangeable, so the rank of Tobs among these B+1 values is uniform. Hence, without ties, the test is valid at every level:

Pr(pcloser≤kB+1)≤kB+1
(21)

The smallest attainable value is 1/(B+1)=1/2001≈0.0005, so p2≥2/2001≈0.001. Every comparison in this project sits at that floor. The p-value says the difference is real under the model, not that it is large.

Effect size and multiple comparisons

The size of a difference is given by the ratio of the observed statistic to the null mean; a ratio below 1 means closer than the null (used in the period table). Because m=20 comparisons are reported (4 thresholds by 3 nulls, and 4 periods by 2 nulls), a Bonferroni correction multiplies each p-value by 20, giving at most about 20×0.001=0.02. Significance survives; what changes between null models is the direction, not the existence, of the difference.

Dynamical system: how the stock of buildings grew

Let N(t) be the number of buildings built up to year t. A logistic equation, dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K), has two equilibria: N* = 0, unstable because f′(0) = r > 0, and N* = K, stable because f′(K) = −r < 0.

Fitted on 1950–2020, two independent methods agree: r ≈ 0.084 to 0.088 per year and K ≈ 30,000 to 31,100 buildings, with the fastest growth (about 650 buildings per year) around 1997. Growth has been slowing since, as the model predicts near K.

The fit is rough. The R² of 0.998 on the cumulative curve is inflated because any increasing curve looks smooth; the more honest figure is 0.69 for the yearly increments, and the residuals swing in waves that the model cannot explain. K is only 12–16% above the 2020 stock and describes buildings that still stand, so it is not a forecast.

Monte Carlo test of proximity

For each building the statistic is the distance to the nearest residential cell, and the test compares the median with 2,000 random placements under each null model. The empirical p-value is (1 + number of simulated medians at least as extreme) / (2,000 + 1), so it can never be zero. Every comparison below reaches the smallest possible value (p ≤ 0.001); with 27,000 buildings the question is not whether the difference is real but how large and how fragile it is.

What this cannot tell you

  • The data cover the Netherlands, not Brussels, and the whole sector rather than one company. Nothing here measures noise, traffic or effects on residents.
  • Only buildings still standing in 2021 are included, and their logistics function was inferred from 2020 company data. The growth curve describes today’s stock, not the history of the sector.
  • Distances run from a building’s centroid to the centre of a 500 m cell, with population from 2025. Differences below about 500 m should not be interpreted.
  • Towns may have grown around older buildings, so the trend with construction year is not causal.
  • About 16% of buildings dated 1950–2020 fall in years ending in 0, against 10% expected, so some construction years look rounded or estimated.
