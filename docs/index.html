Evaluating the Proximity of Logistics Facilities to Residential Neighbourhoods in the Netherlands Through Dynamical Systems and Monte Carlo Methods

How close are Dutch logistics buildings to the places where people live, and are they closer or farther than if they had been sited at random within industrial estates?

Advanced Mathematics for Business and Technology, Vrije Universiteit Brussel. Individual project. Data: 26,951 logistics buildings (Nefs, 2022) and a 500 m population grid (CBS).

The short answer depends on what “random” means

Median distance from a logistics building to the centre of the nearest 500 m cell with at least 100 inhabitants. Bars show the middle 95% of 2,000 random placements under each null model; the diamond is the observed value.

The verdict flips with the null model

Against estates chosen by area, logistics sits closer to residents than chance. Against estates that are all equally likely, it sits farther (for 5, 100 and 500 inhabitants per cell). A plain “closer than random” is therefore not justified.

Position inside an estate barely matters

Keeping each building in its real estate and moving it at random changes the median by only about 2% (8 m), far below the 500 m resolution of the grid. Proximity is mostly decided by which estate hosts logistics.

Newer buildings are farther from today’s homes

The median distance rises from 286 m for buildings from before 1970 to 512 m for those from 2005 on. This holds against both random baselines, but it describes the buildings that exist now; it does not show that planners chose to move logistics away.

Dynamical system: how the stock of buildings grew

Let N(t) be the number of buildings built up to year t. A logistic equation, dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K), has two equilibria: N* = 0, unstable because f′(0) = r > 0, and N* = K, stable because f′(K) = −r < 0.

Fitted on 1950–2020, two independent methods agree: r ≈ 0.084 to 0.088 per year and K ≈ 30,000 to 31,100 buildings, with the fastest growth (about 650 buildings per year) around 1997. Growth has been slowing since, as the model predicts near K.

The fit is rough. The R² of 0.998 on the cumulative curve is inflated because any increasing curve looks smooth; the more honest figure is 0.69 for the yearly increments, and the residuals swing in waves that the model cannot explain. K is only 12–16% above the 2020 stock and describes buildings that still stand, so it is not a forecast.

Monte Carlo test of proximity

For each building the statistic is the distance to the nearest residential cell, and the test compares the median with 2,000 random placements under each null model. The empirical p-value is (1 + number of simulated medians at least as extreme) / (2,000 + 1), so it can never be zero. Every comparison below reaches the smallest possible value (p ≤ 0.001); with 27,000 buildings the question is not whether the difference is real but how large and how fragile it is.

The mathematics in brief

Stability by linearisation. Near an equilibrium N*, write u = N − N*. Then du/dt ≈ f′(N*)u, so u(t) = u0ef′(N*)t: disturbances shrink if f′(N*) < 0 and grow if it is positive. In one dimension f′(N*) is the only eigenvalue of the Jacobian.

N(t) = K / (1 + e−r(t − t0)), with maximal growth rK/4 at N = K/2

Monte Carlo. By the law of large numbers a simulated mean converges to its expectation, and by the central limit theorem its error shrinks like σ/√B for B replicates: four times as many replicates halve the error.

What this cannot tell you

  • The data cover the whole sector rather than one company. Nothing here measures noise, traffic or effects on residents.
  • Only buildings still standing in 2021 are included, and their logistics function was inferred from 2020 company data. The growth curve describes today’s stock, not the history of the sector.
  • Distances run from a building’s centroid to the centre of a 500 m cell, with population from 2025. Differences below about 500 m should not be interpreted.
  • Towns may have grown around older buildings, so the trend with construction year is not causal.
  • About 16% of buildings dated 1950–2020 fall in years ending in 0, against 10% expected, so some construction years look rounded or estimated.
